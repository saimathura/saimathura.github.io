There is a weight you carry when you believe everything matters.

It sits on your chest the moment you wake up — before the alarm, before the coffee, before you have even opened your eyes. It is the weight of *significance*. The silent, suffocating belief that every single thing you do today must count. That every hour not spent climbing toward some shimmering, distant version of "success" is an hour wasted. That you are, at this very moment, falling behind.

You know the feeling, don't you?

You set goals — ambitious, towering, beautiful goals — and then you watch yourself stumble toward them like a person running through wet sand. You tick off one task and three more appear. You finish one chapter and the book grows longer. You solve one equation and the universe, indifferent as ever, hands you twelve more. And somewhere in the middle of all that striving, a thought starts whispering from the back of your skull, the one you're not supposed to say out loud:

*"I am failing at this. And I am tired."*

If you have felt that — if you are feeling it right now, reading this on a screen with tired eyes and a coffee going cold beside you — then I wrote this for you.

This is not an academic lecture on philosophy. This is not a self-help listicle. This is the closest thing I can offer to a hug through a screen. And I promise, by the time you reach the end, your shoulders will be a little lower.

## I. The Weight of Significance

We are raised on a diet of purpose.

From the moment we are old enough to understand language, we are told that we are *meant for something*. Parents whisper it over cribs. Teachers write it on report cards. Society builds entire cathedrals — literal and metaphorical — around the idea that your life must *mean* something. That there is a plan. That there is a destination. That significance is not just possible but *required*.

And so we carry it. We carry "God." We carry "Legacy." We carry "Potential." We carry these words like stones in a backpack, and we wonder why our knees hurt by the time we are twenty-five.

I carried them, too.

I was the person who set twelve goals before breakfast. I was the person who measured a day's worth by its output — papers read, problems solved, tasks completed. If the list wasn't empty by midnight, the day was a failure, and by extension, so was I. I was building my identity out of achievement, brick by brick, and I did not notice the walls were closing in.

The guilt was the worst part. Not guilt for doing something wrong, but guilt for *not doing enough*. For resting. For watching a film instead of reading a paper. For being human instead of being a machine. There was always a quiet voice saying: *You could be doing more. You should be doing more. Everyone else is doing more.*

That voice, I have learned, is the voice of significance. And it is a liar.

## II. Just Be a Rock

I first watched *Everything Everywhere All at Once* in 2023, during my college days. I sat in a room with friends, watched a woman fight interdimensional battles with googly eyes and hot-dog fingers, and thought: *Well, that was weird and fun.*

I did not cry. I did not think about it for weeks. I did not understand.

Because back then, I did not have the vocabulary for what I was feeling. I did not know the word *existentialism*. I did not know the word *absurdism*. I did not know that the weight on my chest had a name, or that there were entire libraries of human thought dedicated to the question of why it was there.

Then, recently, I watched it again.

And I wept for the last thirty-five minutes straight.

Not the polite, single-tear kind of crying. The kind where your chest heaves and your vision blurs and something inside you that has been held very tightly for a very long time finally, mercifully, lets go. I did not fully understand why I was crying, only that it felt like breathing after being underwater.

Let me tell you about the scene that broke me.

There is a moment in the film — after the kung fu and the multiverse chaos and the everything-bagel of nothingness — where two characters sit on the edge of a canyon. They are not people anymore. They are rocks. Just two rocks, sitting on a cliff, looking out at the sky. No dialogue. No soundtrack. No conflict. Just silence and the slow crawl of shadows across stone.

In any other film, this would be a joke. But here, after an hour of watching characters fight desperately to matter in an infinite multiverse, it is the most profound scene in the movie. Because the rocks are at peace. The rocks have no goals. The rocks have no deadlines, no equations to solve, no career milestones to hit. The rocks just *are*.

And when Joy — the daughter who has stared into the void and decided that nothing matters — sits there as a rock, and Evelyn — the mother who has fought across universes to save her — sits beside her, also as a rock, what they are really saying is:

*You are allowed to be small. You are allowed to just sit in the sun.*

That is what made me cry. Not sadness. Not grief. *Permission*.

Permission to stop. Permission to be still. Permission to exist without justifying my existence with productivity or achievement or some grand cosmic purpose. The film did not give me an answer to the meaning of life. It gave me something better: the possibility that the question itself was optional.

And then, in the final act, when everything collapses and the universe offers its verdict, Evelyn says the words that shattered me:

*"We can do whatever we want. Nothing matters."*

She says it not with despair, but with tenderness. Not as a death sentence, but as a liberation. And in that moment, something clicked — a key turning in a lock I did not know existed — and I understood what I had been starving for.

Not motivation. Not discipline. Not another goal or another plan.

A hug. A cosmic, philosophical, warm-blanket-on-a-cold-night *hug* that says: You do not have to earn the right to exist. You already do.

## III. The Name for What I Needed

The philosophy I was reaching for — blindly, tearfully, in the glow of a screen at 2 a.m. — has a name.

**Optimistic Nihilism.**

It sounds like a contradiction. *Nihilism* conjures images of brooding figures in black turtlenecks declaring that life is meaningless while staring into the rain. It is the philosophy of the void — the cold, clinical recognition that the universe does not care about you, your goals, your suffering, or your dreams. On a cosmic scale, you are a temporary arrangement of atoms on a pale blue dot, and when those atoms scatter, the universe will not pause to notice.

That is terrifying, if you stop there.

But Optimistic Nihilism does not stop there. It takes that terrifying premise — *nothing inherently matters* — and performs a quiet, radical act of alchemy. It flips the coin. Because if nothing *inherently* matters, then nothing *has to* matter. Your failures do not matter. Your embarrassments do not matter. That presentation you fumbled, that exam you bombed, that relationship that ended — the void swallows them all equally, along with every triumph and every tragedy since the first stars ignited.

And if the void eats everything, then you are free.

Free to choose what matters *to you*. Free to build meaning like a sandcastle — knowing the tide will come, building it anyway, because the building is the point. Free to pursue your work not because the universe demands it, but because *you* find it interesting. Free to fail, because failure on a cosmic scale is indistinguishable from success.

This is the shift: from *"nothing matters, so why try?"* to *"nothing matters, so why not just be?"*

Albert Camus captured this perfectly in *The Myth of Sisyphus*. Sisyphus, condemned by the gods to push a boulder up a hill for eternity, only to watch it roll back down each time, is the ultimate image of futility. But Camus does not ask us to pity Sisyphus. He asks us to imagine him *happy*. Because if Sisyphus can look at that boulder — his pointless, eternal, absurd task — and choose to find meaning in the pushing itself, then the gods have lost. The punishment becomes a practice. The struggle becomes the point.

"One must imagine Sisyphus happy," Camus wrote. And sitting on my floor, tears drying on my face after watching a film about a laundromat owner fighting a multiverse, I thought: *One must imagine the rock at peace.*

## IV. The God-Shaped Hole

I need to tell you something I have not told many people.

I lost my faith.

I do not say this dramatically, the way characters in films lose their faith — in a single, cinematic moment of crisis, falling to their knees in a storm, shaking their fist at the sky. It was quieter than that. Slower. Like a colour fading from a photograph left too long in the sun.

One day I believed. The next day I still believed, but less. And then there was a morning when I tried to pray and felt… nothing. Not anger, not rebellion, just a vast, polite emptiness, like knocking on a door and realizing no one is home.

What followed was not freedom. It was guilt.

A strange, specific, corrosive guilt — the guilt of *letting go*. As though by ceasing to believe, I had betrayed something. Betrayed *someone*. Every time I tried to reach back toward faith, to hold onto that old comfort, I felt like an imposter. Like my hands were dirty. I could not explain why.

I still cannot, fully. It is one of those feelings that lives in the body, not the mind — a tightness in the chest, a flinch when someone mentions prayer, a quiet shame that has no clear source. If you have felt it, you know exactly what I mean. If you have not, I envy you.

The fear was this: without God, without the Big Observer, without someone *watching* and *keeping score*, the world felt cold. Unwitnessed. If no one is writing the story, then the story has no plot, and I am not a character but a scribble — random marks on a page that no one will read.

That fear sat in me for months.

And then the universe did something strange.

## V. A Gift from the Void

I am going to tell you a story now, and I need you to understand: I am not claiming this as evidence of anything. Not God. Not destiny. Not cosmic intelligence. I am simply telling you what happened, and what it meant to me.

During one of my lowest stretches — when faith felt like a locked room and the future felt like a fog — I came across a reel on Instagram. It was one of those short, earnest clips about "the universe" that the algorithm serves you at 1 a.m. when you have been scrolling for too long. The person in the reel said something like: *"If you ask the universe for something, it gives it to you."*

I know how that sounds. I know. It sounds like the kind of thing printed on a candle at a bookshop that sells crystals. But I was tired, and I was lonely, and I was grasping at anything — any thread, any whisper — that might make the world feel less empty.

So I did something dumb. Wonderfully, beautifully dumb.

I looked up at the ceiling of my room and said, out loud, to no one in particular:

*"Okay, universe. Give me a gift. You have twenty-four hours."*

I felt ridiculous. I went to sleep.

The next day, I got a message on Instagram from my friend's cousin — a person I had not spoken to in months. She had gone to Singapore. She had bought me a gift. She wanted to know where to send it.

I sat there, staring at my phone, and I laughed. Not because I had proven the existence of a cosmic delivery service, but because the timing was so absurd, so perfectly improbable, that it felt like the universe winking at me. Like the chaos had momentarily arranged itself into a shape that looked like kindness.

Here is what I took from that moment: I do not need a Grand Plan to experience wonder. I do not need a God keeping score to feel connected. The world is random, and sometimes randomness is warm. Sometimes the void sends you a text message from Singapore.

Friedrich Nietzsche — who declared the death of God over a century ago and spent the rest of his career figuring out what to do with the funeral — wrote about something he called *amor fati*: the love of fate. Not just acceptance of what happens to you, but a deep, radical love for it. Every stumble, every loss, every improbable gift from a friend's cousin. Loving the chaos *because* it is chaos, not in spite of it.

In *The Gay Science*, Nietzsche wrote: *"I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things; then I shall be one of those who make things beautiful."*

That reel, that message, that ridiculous twenty-four-hour experiment — it did not make me religious again. But it made me *open* again. Open to the possibility that meaning does not have to be handed down from above. It can float in from the side, unexpected, wearing a Singapore souvenir.

## VI. The Library of the Void — Books That Hold You

If you have read this far, I suspect you are the kind of person who finds comfort in knowing that others have walked this road before you. So let me introduce you to a few friends of mine — writers who have stared into the same void and come back with something useful.

**Albert Camus — *The Myth of Sisyphus*** (1942)

I mentioned Sisyphus earlier, and Camus is the reason we imagine him happy. This slim, fierce essay is the foundational text of Absurdism — the philosophy that says the universe is meaningless, humans desperately seek meaning, and the collision between the two is *absurd*. But instead of surrendering to that absurdity, Camus says: *revolt*. Keep pushing the boulder. Not because it will stay at the top, but because the pushing is yours.

If you feel crushed under the weight of your own goals, Camus will not lighten the load. He will teach you to love the weight.

**Wendy Syfret — *The Sunny Nihilist: A Declaration of the Pleasure of Pointlessness*** (2023)

If Camus is the stern professor of the void, Syfret is the warm friend who brings tea. Her book is a direct, accessible exploration of what happens when you stop demanding that life be significant. She argues — gently, persuasively — that the pressure to find meaning is itself the source of most modern anxiety, and that embracing pointlessness can be a doorway to genuine pleasure. This is the book I wish I had read before my breakdown on the floor.

**Ron Currie — *Everything Matters!*** (2009)

A novel about a man who is told, from before birth, that the world will end on a specific date. He knows the expiration date of everything — every love, every friendship, every sunset. And yet the novel is not about despair. It is about how knowing that everything ends makes everything *brighter*. If you have ever wondered whether meaning is possible in a finite universe, this book will convince you that finitude is the very thing that makes meaning burn so fiercely.

**David Landers — *Optimistic Nihilism*** (2021)

Landers is a psychologist who lost his religion and found something in its place. His book is part memoir, part philosophical manifesto, tracing his journey from devout belief to secular clarity. If you, like me, are struggling with the guilt of letting go of God, Landers will sit beside you on that canyon ledge and tell you it is okay to be a rock for a while.

**Friedrich Nietzsche — *The Gay Science*** (1882)

Yes, Nietzsche. Not the caricature — not the angry moustache man misquoted by people on the internet — but the real Nietzsche, who was funny, and lonely, and deeply human. *The Gay Science* is where he introduces the death of God and *amor fati*, and it is where the modern conversation about meaning without divinity begins. Read it slowly. Let it argue with you.

**Brittney L. Hartley — *No Nonsense Spirituality: All the Tools No Belief Required***

For those of us who left religion but miss the *feeling* — the ritual, the stillness, the sense of being part of something — Hartley offers a toolkit. No dogma, no deity, just practice. This is the manual for building a spiritual life on your own terms.

These books are not scripture. They are not prescriptions. They are conversations — voices from across centuries, sitting with you, saying: *You are not the first person to feel this way. You will not be the last. And here is what we found when we looked into the dark.*

## VII. The Waymond Strategy — How to Work Without Weight

Here is the practical part. Because philosophy that does not change how you live your Monday morning is just decoration.

You have goals. I have goals. We are not going to abandon them — Optimistic Nihilism is not about sitting on a hill doing nothing for the rest of your life (though you are allowed to do that sometimes). It is about changing the *energy* with which you pursue those goals. It is about working without the crushing terror of failure.

In *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, there are two strategies for dealing with the multiverse's chaos.

**Evelyn's strategy (before her transformation):** Fight harder. Be more. Control everything. Set bigger goals, work longer hours, grip tighter. This is the strategy most of us use. It is the strategy of *significance* — the belief that if we just push hard enough, we can force the universe to cooperate.

It does not work. It leads to exhaustion, resentment, and crying on the floor at 2 a.m. (speaking from experience).

**Waymond's strategy:** Be kind. Be gentle. Put googly eyes on things. Do the laundry. File the taxes. Fight the chaos not with force but with *softness*. This is, I would argue, the most radical act in the entire film — a man in a collapsing multiverse choosing to be tender.

Waymond does not achieve less than Evelyn. He achieves *differently*. He moves through the chaos with open hands instead of clenched fists. And when everything falls apart, he is the one still standing — not because he is stronger, but because he was never rigid enough to break.

This is the Waymond Strategy for your goals:

**The old way:** *"I must finish this project perfectly, on time, or I am a failure and my career is over and I have wasted my potential."*

Result: Paralysis. Fear. Procrastination disguised as perfectionism. The work becomes a threat.

**The Waymond way:** *"This is a puzzle. It is one of a billion puzzles in a billion worlds. If I do not solve it today, the sun still rises tomorrow. The stars still burn. I am still here. I will try again."*

Result: The stakes drop to zero. The tension dissolves. You sit down to work not because you are terrified of the consequences of *not* working, but because the work itself is interesting. And paradoxically — beautifully, maddeningly — when you stop fearing the failure, you become *better* at the work. Your brain relaxes. Your creativity opens up. You solve the problem not because your life depends on it, but because solving problems is what you enjoy.

This is the gift of Optimistic Nihilism, applied:

You can miss a deadline.

You can fail an exam.

You can write a terrible first draft.

You can submit a paper that gets rejected.

You can spend an entire Sunday doing absolutely nothing.

And the world keeps spinning. The void does not judge you. You are still worthy of love, of joy, of googly eyes on your problems.

## VIII. The Hug

We are near the end now, and I want to return to where we started.

I told you that I wanted someone to hug me and say that there was no pressure. That I could be a rock. That I could fail and still be whole. I wanted someone to hold me the way Evelyn finally holds Joy — not with the grip of a parent trying to control, but with the open arms of a person who has seen the void and decided that love is the only sane response to it.

I cannot reach through this screen. I cannot sit beside you on a canyon ledge. But I can tell you what I have learned, from the bottom of a very human, very imperfect heart:

You are not a failure for struggling. You are a person. Struggling is what people do. It is not a defect; it is a feature.

You are not guilty for losing your faith. Faith is not a loyalty test. If it left, it left for a reason, and that reason does not have to be your fault.

You are not behind. There is no schedule. There is no cosmic timetable with your name on it, no divine checkpoint you were supposed to reach by age twenty-five or thirty or sixty. There is just today, and what you choose to do with it.

And if today you choose to do nothing — if today you choose to be a rock, sitting in the sun, existing without justification — then today is not wasted. Today is the point.

Take a breath. A real one. Fill your lungs.

Good.

Now let your shoulders drop.

*We can do whatever we want. Nothing matters.*

And isn't that wonderful?

## A Final Note

I want to be honest with you, because this essay has been nothing if not honest.

I am not here to argue that Optimistic Nihilism is *the* way to live. I am not starting a movement, recruiting followers, or selling a course. Philosophy is not a religion; it is a toolbox, and different tools work for different hands.

But for people like me — people who hold weight and value to *everything*, who treat every task as a test and every day as a judgment — this particular tool has been a lifeline. It loosened the grip. It lowered the stakes. It let me breathe.

If you are someone who carries the weight of significance on your shoulders — if you set ambitious goals and then punish yourself for not meeting them, if you feel guilty for resting, if the word "enough" sounds like a foreign language — then I am writing this directly to you.

I am here to hug you and say:

*We can do whatever we want.*

*Nothing matters.*

Now go be a rock for a while. The goals will be there when you get back. And when you return to them, you will find they are lighter than you remember.
